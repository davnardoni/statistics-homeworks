Homework 7 — Convergence and Bayesian Inference

Modes of convergence, the Law of Large Numbers, the Central Limit Theorem, conditional probability, and Bayesian statistical inference.

Part I — Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

1. Main Modes of Convergence

In probability theory, convergence describes how a sequence of random variables approaches a limiting random variable. Different modes of convergence specify different meanings of this approximation.

1.1 Convergence in probability

A sequence of random variables \(X_n\) converges in probability to \(X\) if, for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), the probability that \(X_n\) differs from \(X\) by more than \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero.

\[ X_n \xrightarrow{P} X \iff \forall\varepsilon>0,\quad P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\to 0 \]

This means that large deviations from the limit become increasingly unlikely. It is particularly important in statistics because it is the usual notion of consistency for estimators.

1.2 Almost sure convergence

Almost sure convergence means that, with probability one, the sequence of observed values converges to the limiting random variable.

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X \iff P\left( \lim_{n\to\infty}X_n=X \right)=1 \]

This is stronger than convergence in probability. The sequence converges along almost every possible outcome, although exceptions on a set of probability zero are allowed.

1.3 Mean-square convergence (L²)

Mean-square convergence requires the expected squared difference between \(X_n\) and \(X\) to approach zero. The relevant second moments must be finite.

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X \iff E[(X_n-X)^2]\to 0 \]

This mode controls the average squared estimation error. It is useful when studying the mean squared error of estimators. By Markov's inequality applied to the squared error, L² convergence implies convergence in probability.

1.4 Convergence in distribution

Convergence in distribution occurs when the cumulative distribution functions of \(X_n\) converge to the distribution function of \(X\) at every continuity point of the limiting distribution function.

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{d}X \iff F_{X_n}(t)\to F_X(t) \] \[ \text{for every continuity point }t \text{ of }F_X \]

It describes convergence of probability distributions rather than necessarily the observed values themselves. This mode is fundamental to the Central Limit Theorem.

1.5 Relationships between convergence modes

The main implications are:

\[ X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X \implies X_n\xrightarrow{P}X \] \[ X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X \implies X_n\xrightarrow{P}X \] \[ X_n\xrightarrow{P}X \implies X_n\xrightarrow{d}X \]

In general, the reverse implications do not hold. Almost sure convergence and L² convergence do not imply one another without additional assumptions. However, convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to that constant.

Mode What converges? Main statistical role
In probability Probability of large errors Estimator consistency; Weak LLN
Almost surely Values for almost every outcome Strong LLN
Mean-square (L²) Expected squared error Mean squared error analysis
In distribution Cumulative distribution functions CLT; asymptotic inference

2. Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

Consider independent and identically distributed random variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\), with finite mean \(\mu=E[X_i]\). Their sample mean is:

\[ \overline X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i \]

Weak Law of Large Numbers

The Weak LLN states that the sample mean converges in probability to the population mean.

\[ \overline X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu \]

If the observations also have finite variance \(\sigma^2\), independence gives:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \] \[ P(|\overline X_n-\mu|\geq\varepsilon) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0 \]

The second inequality is Chebyshev's inequality. It provides a direct proof of the Weak LLN under the finite-variance assumption.

Strong Law of Large Numbers

The Strong LLN states that the sample mean converges almost surely to the population mean. For i.i.d. observations, a finite absolute first moment is sufficient.

\[ E[|X_1|]<\infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \overline X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu \]

Therefore, the Weak LLN guarantees convergence in probability, while the Strong LLN guarantees almost sure convergence.

LLN and L² convergence

Under the finite-variance i.i.d. assumptions, the sample mean also converges in mean square:

\[ E[(\overline X_n-\mu)^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0 \]

Thus, the same sample mean can satisfy several modes of convergence simultaneously, depending on the assumptions.

3. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The classical CLT describes the limiting distribution of a properly standardized sample mean. For i.i.d. observations with finite mean \(\mu\) and finite positive variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1) \]

Unlike the LLN, which describes convergence of the sample mean to the population mean, the CLT describes the distribution of the estimation error after scaling by \(\sqrt n\).

The limiting normal distribution makes it possible to approximate sampling probabilities, construct confidence intervals, and perform hypothesis tests for sufficiently large samples.

LLN versus CLT

The LLN explains why the sample mean approaches the true mean. The CLT explains the approximate distribution of its fluctuations around that mean. Convergence in probability is central to the Weak LLN, almost sure convergence to the Strong LLN, and convergence in distribution to the CLT.

4. Why Does the Factor √n Appear?

The factor \(\sqrt n\) arises from the behavior of variance when independent random variables are added.

For independent observations with common variance \(\sigma^2\), covariance terms vanish:

\[ \operatorname{Var} \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = n\sigma^2 \]

Consequently, the standard deviation of the sum is \(\sigma\sqrt n\), while the variance and standard deviation of the sample mean are:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \] \[ \operatorname{SD}(\overline X_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \]

Therefore, the typical size of the sample mean's fluctuations is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\). Multiplication by \(\sqrt n\) produces a standardized quantity with non-vanishing variance:

\[ \operatorname{Var} \left(\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)\right) = \sigma^2 \]

Independence is sufficient for variance additivity, although zero covariance is enough. With dependent observations, covariance terms must also be considered:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j) \]

Depending on the strength of dependence, these covariance terms may change the variance growth and the appropriate convergence rate.

Role in statistical inference

Under the classical i.i.d. assumptions, the standard error of the sample mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). In practice, the unknown population standard deviation is commonly estimated using the sample standard deviation.

\[ SE(\overline X_n) \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]

The \(\sqrt n\) rate is fundamental to the asymptotic distribution of many estimators. Increasing the sample size by a factor of four approximately halves the standard error when the usual assumptions apply.

Part II — Conditional Probability and Bayesian Inference

5. Conditional Probability

In probability theory, conditional probability measures the probability of an event A when another event B is known to have occurred. For \(P(B)>0\):

\[ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} \]

If A and B are independent, knowing B does not change the probability of A:

\[ P(A\mid B)=P(A) \]

In statistics, conditional probabilities are used to describe relationships between variables and construct statistical models. In particular, a likelihood function describes the probability or density of observed data conditional on a parameter or hypothesis.

6. Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning direction, allowing us to update the probability of a hypothesis after observing evidence.

\[ P(H\mid D) = \frac{P(D\mid H)P(H)}{P(D)} \]

Here, H is a hypothesis and D represents the observed evidence.

The three fundamental components

Component Meaning Role
Prior \(P(H)\) Belief before observing the data
Likelihood \(P(D\mid H)\) How compatible the data are with H
Posterior \(P(H\mid D)\) Updated belief after observing the data

The denominator \(P(D)\) is the evidence, or marginal likelihood. It normalizes the posterior so that probabilities sum to one.

\[ \text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood}\times\text{Prior} \]

For a continuous parameter \(\theta\), Bayesian updating is written using probability densities:

\[ \pi(\theta\mid D) = \frac{ L(\theta;D)\pi(\theta) }{ \int L(u;D)\pi(u)\,du } \]

This updating process is central to Bayesian statistical inference because it combines prior information with observed evidence to obtain posterior distributions for unknown quantities.

7. Example 1 — Diagnostic Test

Consider a diagnostic test for a disease. The prior probability is the disease prevalence. The likelihood of a positive test depends on whether the disease is present.

\[ P(D\mid +) = \frac{ P(+\mid D)P(D) }{ P(+\mid D)P(D) + P(+\mid D^c)P(D^c) } \]

Here \(P(+\mid D)\) is sensitivity, while \(P(+\mid D^c)=1-\text{specificity}\) is the false-positive probability.

This example illustrates the base-rate effect: even a highly sensitive test may have a relatively low posterior probability of disease when the initial prevalence is small.

Diagnostic testing is an application of Bayesian updating. Its conclusions depend on the prior prevalence and on the assumed accuracy of the test.

8. Example 2 — Estimating a Coin's Probability

Suppose a coin has an unknown probability \(\theta\) of producing heads. We observe h heads and t tails, with \(n=h+t\).

Assuming independent Bernoulli trials with a common probability \(\theta\), the binomial likelihood is proportional to:

\[ L(\theta;h,t) \propto \theta^h(1-\theta)^t \]

Choose a Beta prior distribution:

\[ \theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a,b) \] \[ \pi(\theta)\propto \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1} \]

Multiplying the likelihood by the prior produces another Beta distribution:

\[ \theta\mid h,t \sim \operatorname{Beta}(a+h,b+t) \]

The corresponding prior and posterior means are:

\[ E[\theta]=\frac{a}{a+b} \] \[ E[\theta\mid h,t] = \frac{a+h}{a+b+h+t} \]

Beta-Binomial updating calculator

The Beta prior represents information about the probability of heads before observing the data. The binomial likelihood summarizes the evidence provided by the observed heads and tails. The posterior combines both sources of information.

With a small sample, the prior can substantially influence the posterior. As more independent observations are collected, the likelihood generally becomes increasingly influential.

9. Applications, Advantages and Limitations

Applications

Bayesian inference is used in diagnostic testing, parameter estimation, forecasting, and decision-making under uncertainty. Prior distributions can incorporate information from previous studies or expert knowledge, while likelihood functions connect statistical models to newly observed data.

Advantages

Bayesian methods provide a systematic framework for updating uncertainty as new information becomes available. They allow previous information to be incorporated explicitly and produce posterior distributions for unknown parameters.

Limitations

Posterior conclusions may be sensitive to the chosen prior, especially with limited data. Incorrect likelihood models or violated independence assumptions can lead to misleading results. Some Bayesian models also require computationally demanding posterior calculations.

Final Conclusion

The modes of convergence explain different aspects of the limiting behavior of random variables. The LLN establishes the consistency of sample averages, while the CLT characterizes their asymptotic fluctuations and explains the importance of the square-root sample-size rate.

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem provide the mathematical basis for Bayesian inference. The posterior combines the prior with the likelihood, so statistical conclusions depend on both the observed data and the assumptions used to model them.
