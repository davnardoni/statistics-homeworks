Homework 6 — Statistical Independence

Constructing contingency tables, computing marginal and conditional frequencies, and investigating statistical independence using the Chi-square test.

1. Theoretical Background

Independence in probability

Two random variables X and F are independent if knowing the value of one variable does not change the probability distribution of the other. For discrete random variables, independence requires:

\[ P(X=x_i,F=f_j) = P(X=x_i)P(F=f_j) \]

Equivalently, whenever the conditioning event has positive probability:

\[ P(F=f_j\mid X=x_i)=P(F=f_j) \]

Independence in statistics

In statistics, independence is investigated using observed data. A contingency table summarizes the joint frequencies of two categorical variables. If the variables are independent, the joint relative frequencies should be approximately equal to the products of the corresponding marginal relative frequencies.

The Chi-square test of independence evaluates whether the differences between observed and expected frequencies provide evidence against the hypothesis of independence.

\[ H_0: X \text{ and } F \text{ are independent} \] \[ H_1: X \text{ and } F \text{ are dependent} \]

3. Contingency Table

The observed frequency \(O_{ij}\) counts the observations belonging to category \(x_i\) and category \(f_j\).

Marginal relative frequencies

Marginal frequencies describe the distribution of each variable independently of the other.

\[ p_{i\cdot}=\frac{O_{i\cdot}}{n}, \qquad p_{\cdot j}=\frac{O_{\cdot j}}{n} \]

Conditional relative frequencies

Conditional frequencies describe the distribution of one variable for a fixed category of the other.

\[ P(F=f_j\mid X=x_i) = \frac{O_{ij}}{O_{i\cdot}} \] \[ P(X=x_i\mid F=f_j) = \frac{O_{ij}}{O_{\cdot j}} \]

4. Expected Frequencies Under Independence

Under the null hypothesis of independence, each expected absolute frequency is calculated by multiplying the corresponding row and column totals and dividing by the sample size.

\[ E_{ij} = \frac{O_{i\cdot}O_{\cdot j}}{n} \]

5. Chi-square Test of Independence

The Pearson Chi-square statistic measures the discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies:

\[ \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \]

The degrees of freedom are: \((r-1)(c-1)\), where r and c represent the numbers of categories of X and F.

Significance level: \(\alpha=0.05\). The Chi-square approximation is generally considered reliable when expected cell frequencies are sufficiently large.

6. Interpretation and Conclusion

Conclusion

Statistical association does not imply causality. Even when two variables are associated, this does not establish that one causes the other. Other variables or confounding factors may explain the observed relationship.
