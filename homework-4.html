Homework 4 — Welford's Algorithm and Sampling Statistics

Deriving numerically stable recurrence formulas for the mean and variance, and investigating their statistical properties through Monte Carlo simulation.

Exercise 1 — Welford's Recurrence Formulas

1. Objective

The goal is to derive recurrence formulas for the sum of squared deviations and the sample variance, following the same incremental approach used by Knuth's recurrence formula for the mean.

The main advantage is that we can update the mean and variance whenever a new observation arrives, without storing all previous observations.

2. Incremental mean

Let x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ be a sequence of observations. Knuth's recurrence formula updates the mean as follows:

μₙ = μₙ₋₁ + (xₙ − μₙ₋₁) / n

Define δ = xₙ − μₙ₋₁. Then the new mean is obtained by adding δ/n to the previous mean.

3. Derivation of Welford's sum of squares

Define the sum of squared deviations from the current mean:

SSₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ − μₙ)²

We separate the last observation from the previous n − 1 observations:

SSₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ (xᵢ − μₙ)² + (xₙ − μₙ)²

For the first n − 1 observations, write xᵢ − μₙ = (xᵢ − μₙ₋₁) + (μₙ₋₁ − μₙ). Expanding the square, the cross term disappears because the deviations from the previous mean sum to zero.

SSₙ = SSₙ₋₁ + (n − 1)(μₙ − μₙ₋₁)² + (xₙ − μₙ)²

Using μₙ − μₙ₋₁ = δ/n and xₙ − μₙ = δ(n − 1)/n, we obtain:

SSₙ = SSₙ₋₁ + δ²(n − 1)/n

Since xₙ − μₙ = δ(n − 1)/n, the recurrence can also be written in Welford's standard form:

SSₙ = SSₙ₋₁ + (xₙ − μₙ₋₁)(xₙ − μₙ)

4. Recurrence formula for variance

The unbiased sample variance divides the sum of squares by n − 1:

s²ₙ = SSₙ / (n − 1), for n ≥ 2

Substituting the recurrence for SS gives the equivalent direct update:

s²ₙ = [(n − 2)s²ₙ₋₁ + δ²(n − 1)/n] / (n − 1)

For n ≥ 3, this can be simplified to:

s²ₙ = [(n − 2)/(n − 1)]s²ₙ₋₁ + δ²/n

In practice, Welford's algorithm keeps the running sum of squares and computes the sample variance from it. This avoids unnecessary repeated divisions.

5. Implementation

The essential update requires only three statements, once n has been incremented: 

const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / n;
SS += delta * (x - mean);

Initialize n = 0, mean = 0 and SS = 0. At the end, the sample variance is SS / (n − 1), provided n ≥ 2.

Exercise 1 — Conclusion

Welford's algorithm computes the mean and sample variance in a single pass, using constant memory and O(n) total time. It is generally more numerically stable than computing variance as the difference between the mean of the squares and the square of the mean, especially when observations have a large common offset.

Exercise 2 — Sampling Mean and Variance

1. Experimental model

We consider m independent hackers, each attempting to penetrate n systems. Every attempt produces a score between 0 and 10. For each hacker, the n scores form one sample trajectory.

The scores are independently generated from a fixed binomial distribution with parameters 10 and 0.5. Each score represents the number of successes in 10 independent Bernoulli trials.

X ~ Binomial(10, 0.5)
E[X] = μ = 10 × 0.5 = 5
Var(X) = σ² = 10 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 2.5

For each hacker, we compute the sample mean and unbiased sample variance with Welford's recurrence formulas.

X̄ⱼ = (1/n) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xⱼᵢ
S²ⱼ = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (Xⱼᵢ − X̄ⱼ)²

We then average these two statistics over the m hackers:

Average sample mean = (1/m) Σⱼ₌₁ᵐ X̄ⱼ
Average sample variance = (1/m) Σⱼ₌₁ᵐ S²ⱼ

4. Statistical interpretation

The sample mean and unbiased sample variance are unbiased estimators of the population mean and variance:

E[X̄] = μ = 5
E[S²] = σ² = 2.5

Consequently, averaging the estimates over many independent hackers should produce values close to their theoretical population parameters. Differences are expected because the experiment uses a finite number of random samples.

The variability of the sample mean decreases according to:

Var(X̄) = σ²/n = 2.5/n
SD(X̄) = σ/√n = √(2.5/n)

The unbiased sample variance also becomes more concentrated around the population variance as n increases, although its variability depends on the fourth central moment of the underlying distribution.

For the selected binomial distribution, the fourth central moment is μ₄ = 17.5. Therefore:

Var(S²) = [17.5 − ((n − 3)/(n − 1)) × 6.25] / n

Both statistics become more precise with increasing sample size. This is directly related to the law of large numbers and the consistency of these estimators.

5. Why this is useful in inferential statistics

Inferential statistics uses sample data to estimate unknown population characteristics. The simulation shows that sample statistics can estimate population parameters without observing the entire population.

Unbiasedness ensures that the estimators are correct on average, while decreasing sampling variability makes individual estimates more reliable for large sample sizes. The standard error of the mean is fundamental for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

Exercise 2 — Conclusion

The average of the sample means approaches the theoretical mean μ = 5, and the average of the unbiased sample variances approaches σ² = 2.5 as the number of independent replications increases.

Increasing n reduces the spread of both sampling distributions. The sample means become more tightly concentrated around μ, with standard deviation proportional to 1/√n. The sample variances also become more stable around σ².

These results demonstrate the importance of unbiased, consistent estimators and sample size in statistical inference. Welford's recurrence formulas allow these estimates to be calculated efficiently and accurately even for large streams of observations.
